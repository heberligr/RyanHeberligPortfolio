01

Overview

Generate, Northeastern's student product development studio, is developing a connected pill dispenser with two parts: a home unit and a portable travel unit. I'm on the hardware team, designing the travel unit's mechanism.

The mechanism is a rotating disk of compartments, each holding a 1–4 pill dose. It indexes one compartment at a time to an access opening, where the user takes the dose. It has to do that with one motor, fit in a bag, run on a battery, and know whether pills are actually there.

02

Requirements

5 requirements
  • SizePortable, with a disk about 90 mm across
  • PowerBattery powered: 7.4 V, at most 1 A per phase
  • IndexingRepeatable discrete steps: 45° for 8 compartments
  • HoldingHolds position against bumps in a bag, using no power
  • SensingDetects whether pills are present at the opening
03

Design approach

5 steps
Step 1

BLDC starting point

✕ Can't hold unpowered

The electrical team wanted to use a gimbal BLDC motor. A BLDC can't hold its position without power, so the disk would need a separate way to stay put between doses.

Step 2

Detents

✕ Needed a ~14:1 gearbox

I ran torque and factor-of-safety calculations on a detent hold. Detents strong enough to resist bumps needed more torque to step past than the BLDC could supply under 7.4 V and 1 A, so the design would need a custom ~14:1 printed gearbox.

Step 3

Geneva drive

✕ Added height, still geared

I designed 8- and 7-slot Geneva drives, which lock the disk mechanically between steps with no holding power. They added about 10 mm of height, though, and still needed gear reduction.

Step 4

Worm gearmotor

✓ Recommended

Once the BLDC needed its own gearbox, driver, encoder, and lock, those additions outweighed its benefits. I recommended a self-locking N20 worm gearmotor instead: it holds with no power, has a 10x+ torque margin, and is the thinnest of the options. Magnets and a hall sensor mark each position, so indexing errors can't accumulate from step to step.

Step 5

Mechanical and sensing

→ Prototyping now

The disk and shaft are printed in PLA and run on a 6800 bearing, with an E-clip holding the shaft in place and a PTFE floor under the disk to cut friction. A TAL221 load cell with an overload stop sits at the opening to detect pills. The whole mechanism is modeled parametrically in Onshape.
